Week 7 · Sept 28 – Oct 4 · Ciabattari Ch. 9

Family work and the stalled revolution

Women moved into paid work; men moved into housework far more slowly. Care work is real work, it has a cognitive dimension, and it is shaped by time, power and "doing gender". Policy can reinforce or reduce gender specialisation.

Key points for the multiple choice

What the slides say

Hochschild · Gerson · England

  • Second shift (Hochschild 1989): housework women do after their first shift of paid work; a "leisure gap" at home.
  • Stalled revolution: mismatched expectations about paid and unpaid work.
  • Unfinished revolution (Gerson): young people want egalitarian relationships but doubt they can achieve them.
  • England (2010) optional: the gender revolution is uneven and stalled. Change is asymmetric: women move into "male" jobs, fields and activities; men rarely move into "female" ones, because what is feminine stays devalued and underpaid. Desegregation is a "one-way street".
  • England: "free choice" individualism plus the search for one's "true self" encourages gender essentialism, so women often stay in female-typed fields or subfields. Women entered professions/management more than blue-collar male jobs because they could move up through female jobs with more education.

Separate spheres and care work

  • Separate spheres emerged with the shift from agriculture to industry: men = providers with patriarchal authority; women = private sphere. Cult of domesticity mainly in middle/upper-class white families.
  • Ideal-worker norm: assumes workers have no family responsibilities; reinforced by US policy (limited leave, long hours).
  • Care work: work to maintain a household and care for members: feeding work, housework, consumption work, kin work, childcare. Mostly unpaid and private; taken for granted.
  • Consumption work = monitoring the market, comparison shopping, keeping records and budgets. Kin work = maintaining ties, gifts, gatherings.
  • Care work has a cognitive dimension (deciding dinner, organising). It is both a consequence of family life and a way family identity is created: care work is a way to do family (interactionist view); it has practical and symbolic value.
  • COVID: more women left jobs; women worked from the kitchen with interruptions, men from private offices.

Employment history

  • From the 19th c. families purchased rather than produced goods. Women dominated domestic service, teaching, nursing.
  • White married women had the lowest employment in the 1920s; some earned at home informally, preserving the image of separate spheres.
  • Black women had higher employment and were always excluded from the cult of domesticity (Hill Collins); middle-class Black women had high participation (Landry).
  • Since 1950 three macro changes: decline of manufacturing and stagnant wages (one income no longer enough), removal of legal barriers to "men's" professions, fertility decline after 1964.
  • Many women work part-time involuntarily; leaving work to care "signals a violation of ideal worker norms" (Weisshaar). Men not employed are seen as "failed men".

Housework: three models

  • Women cut housework over 45 years, men doubled theirs; women still do ~60% more.
  • Time availability: women do more because they spend less time in paid work; explains hours but not why domesticity is "women's work".
  • Power-dependency: housework is unpleasant; earnings give power; the more dependent partner does more. Strong evidence but not sufficient.
  • Gender perspective / doing gender (West & Zimmerman; Risman): gender is something we do; housework is ideological and affirms masculinity/femininity. Evidence: men living alone do their own housework until a woman arrives (Gupta); a man's presence creates more work for women (Pepin et al.); when wives out-earn husbands, especially if he is fully dependent, his housework declines.
  • Few people call unequal housework unfair. Macro: men do more housework where women's employment is common and where governments encourage paternity leave (Hook).

Childcare and work–family conflict

  • Both parents spend more time with children today (intensified expectations), sacrificing housework, leisure, time alone.
  • Nonstandard schedules: married fathers do more care while mothers work; single parents face patchwork care (Hepburn).
  • Childcare cost is calculated against the woman's earnings, not as a family expense.
  • Bianchi: high-income families have "too much work", low-income "too little work", middle-income inflexible work.
  • US: individualistic culture → American mothers blamed themselves; European mothers saw external causes and policy solutions. US is the only developed country without paid maternity leave.
  • Motherhood penalty (mothers judged less competent and committed, Correll et al.) vs fatherhood bonus.
  • Policies can reinforce specialisation if only women get leave; leave for fathers improves women's wages. With supportive policies people prefer egalitarian arrangements (Pedulla & Thébaud). Opting out: highly educated stay-at-home mothers reflect unresponsive workplaces (Stone).

Beck & Beck-Gernsheim (2002) optional

  • Women's biographies individualised since the 1960s: education, employment, economic independence, equality rhetoric, work as fulfilment.
  • Gap between equality rhetoric and reality; structural barriers (inflexible jobs, little childcare).
  • Men change more slowly; they accept equality in principle but not in their own homes.
  • Couples live a stalled revolution (Hochschild) or "modernization trap" (Wahl): promises of autonomy vs experiences of withheld recognition.
  • "A job is not only a job, and housework is not only housework": conflicts over housework are about identity, the "conflict behind the conflict".

Ocobock (2025), "Cognitive Labor, Power, and Patriarchal Bargains: Not-so-Invisible Barriers to Gendered Change"

Gender & Society 39(4), 561–589

Cognitive labour is not invisible because of the nature of the work. It only appears invisible to men because they use resistance strategies to avoid it, and women, seeing a gender structure they cannot change, make patriarchal bargains instead of fighting for change.

Main ideas

  • Cognitive labour = the "backstage" thinking, planning, scheduling and organising that makes physical "frontstage" housework happen. Four parts (Daminger): anticipating needs, identifying options, deciding, monitoring results.
  • Women do much more of it; it harms psychological well-being in a way physical care does not.
  • During the pandemic, both partners worked full time from home, so equality was possible, yet the cognitive load (filling and managing children's time, homeschooling schedules) fell on mothers in both unequal and otherwise egalitarian couples. Only 4 of 81 fathers did equal or more.
  • Men avoided it in two ways: non-egalitarian men refused to notice; egalitarian men acknowledged it but "de-gendered" it ("she's a planner, I go with the flow"). Cognitive labour is a "final frontier".
  • Women no longer believed they were naturally better planners; they saw men's "not noticing" as socially allowed avoidance (close to "weaponized incompetence").
  • Rather than fight, women made patriarchal bargains (Kandiyoti): they kept the work to protect their children's well-being and their own emotional well-being.
  • Conclusion: calling cognitive labour "invisible" risks supporting men's power play; change needs men willing to take on the stress of parenting, not only its physical tasks.

Retelling

Ocobock starts from a puzzle. Researchers usually say the mental load stays unequal because it is invisible, yet by 2020 "mental load" memes were everywhere and the pandemic made the problem impossible to miss. So are couples really unaware of it, how do they justify it, and what stops change?

She interviewed 81 heterosexual parents (50 women, 31 men, mostly white, highly educated, liberal) in 2020, all in couples where both partners worked full time from home with children being homeschooled and no outside help. This is a situation that should push couples toward equality.

Physical childcare was already unequal, but the main stress for mothers was something not counted in usual tallies: planning and managing children's endless "dead time". Mothers like Gemma proudly showed detailed schedules; their husbands spoke vaguely of things "falling into place". Even Dave, who scrubbed toilets and admitted his wife's planning was heavier, kept his "go with the flow" role.

Women first used familiar excuses ("I'm just type A") but dropped them: their planning met their children's needs, and their husbands could plan perfectly well at work. They saw the problem as a structure that lets all men ignore this work. Believing they could not change it, they bargained: doing it all was less stressful than begging for help, and protected their children. Ocobock concludes that the invisibility of cognitive labour is produced by gendered power, and that women's exhausted choices should not be blamed on them.

Petersen, Penner & Høgsnes (2014), "From Motherhood Penalties to Husband Premia: Lessons from Norway"

American Journal of Sociology 119(5), 1434–1472

Norway's expanding family policies almost eliminated the motherhood wage penalty, but did nothing to the wage premia men gain from marriage and fatherhood, which is now the harder challenge for gender equality.

Main ideas

  • Family "pulls in opposite directions": it helps men's wages and careers and hurts women's, widening the gender gap.
  • Norwegian family policy is gender-neutral but affects women more; targeted policies (father quotas) aim to strengthen father–child ties.
  • Results: equal pay for equal work by motherhood and marital status was reached within the same occupation and establishment; the decline of the motherhood penalty was "close to sensational".
  • But men keep a small marriage premium and smaller fatherhood premium, and a negative "female main effect" remains.
  • Proposals: split parental leave more evenly (e.g. a third or more for fathers); lower wage inequality and fewer working hours (as in Scandinavia) favour equality. The vanguard of gender equality may have moved from the US to Northern Europe, where the stalled revolution may be "unstalling".

Retelling

Using Norwegian employer–employee data, the authors ask whether mothers and fathers get equal pay for equal work as Norway expanded leave and childcare. Comparing people in the same occupation and workplace, they find the motherhood penalty shrank to almost nothing over a short period. Men's advantages from being husbands and fathers barely moved. So in policy-rich countries the remaining gap is driven by family advantages for men, which policy aimed at mothers cannot reach; more equal leave sharing and shorter, less unequal work are suggested.

UN Women (2019), Families in a Changing World, Ch. 4: Families, income and women's economic security

Families pool and share resources, but having an income of one's own is critical for women's well-being and voice; whether income becomes power depends on the kind of work, household "sharing rules" and social norms.

Main ideas

  • Women's growing access to resources has shifted some power within homes, but gains are uneven across countries and classes.
  • Women entered labour markets under adverse conditions, occupational segregation and a continuing care burden; social protection expansion stalled under austerity.
  • Salary ≠ power: income translates into voice only depending on work type and norms about "sharing rules".
  • Evidence on women's control of resources led cash-transfer programmes to pay mothers.
  • Motherhood employment penalty: in middle/high-income countries young children reduce women's labour participation and raise men's; in low-income countries poverty keeps mothers working.
  • Income gaps between partners come from part-time work, non-standard jobs, pay gaps, segregation and motherhood penalties.

Family lives of older adults

People live longer and more independently, with unequal resources. Help mostly flows down generations, women do most caregiving, and states increasingly push care back to families or markets.

Key points for the multiple choice

What the slides say

Defining old age and demography

  • Caregiving kin feel ambivalence: love and value helping, but costs (tiredness, depression, money, time).
  • Markers: retirement, grandparenthood; 65 as threshold. Three groups: young old (65–74), old old (75–84), oldest old (85+). Age is also "in the eyes of the beholder".
  • At 65 people live another 18–20 years; women live longer but the gap is closing.
  • Most older adults live alone or with a spouse; men more often alone, women more varied arrangements.
  • Poverty fell thanks to Social Security and healthcare, but today's workers risk losing protection (fewer pensions, low wages, strained Social Security).
  • Economic well-being reflects cumulative advantage and disadvantage over the life course by gender, race and class.
  • Foreign-born elders more often live in multigenerational households. Beanpole families: low fertility (fewer siblings) + low mortality (more generations).

Romance in later life

  • More heterogeneous: remarried, widowed, divorced, cohabiting, never married.
  • Why more cohabitation after 50: more unmarried people; prior experience and approval of cohabitation; it is a long-term alternative to marriage, rarely a precursor; it protects existing financial resources.
  • LAT gives companionship without shared-living responsibilities; we don't know how many older Americans are LAT because research uses household-based definitions.

Grandparenting

  • Cherlin & Furstenberg: remote (29%), companionate (55%), involved (16%). Dominant = companionate: time, advice, little discipline or money.
  • The role is not static and differs by grandchild.
  • Conflict between parents and grandparents → more distant grandchildren. After divorce or nonmarital birth, paternal ties weaken and maternal strengthen: matrifocal tilt.
  • Caregiving grandparents (mostly grandmothers). Type one: childcare without co-residence; parents see it as trustworthy, nurturing, cheaper; grandparents feel useful but stressed.
  • Type two, co-residential: multigenerational households or skipped-generation households (grandparent and grandchild without parent: incarceration, substance abuse, illness, death, migration). Custodial grandparents are often economically insecure.
  • Native American grandparents as "cultural conservators". Co-residence grows: longevity, low fertility, costly childcare, Great Recession.

Solidarity, ambivalence, exchanges

  • Intergenerational solidarity = cohesion; dimensions: geographic proximity, contact, emotional closeness, shared values, instrumental and financial exchange.
  • Intergenerational ambivalence = mixed feelings in these relations.
  • Typology: tight-knit (high on all); sociable (close, contact, values, no functional exchange); obligatory (close and in contact, some help, but no emotional closeness or shared values); intimate but distant (emotionally close, shared values, but far, little contact or help); detached (low on all).
  • Closeness and exchange can also intensify conflict. Mother–daughter ties closer; African-American and Latino families more proximity and practical help; white families more financial help.
  • Functional solidarity: instrumental help (childcare, errands, repairs, personal care) and financial support.
  • Most help flows down generations; it reverses only at the oldest ages. Lower-income parents give more instrumental help, higher-income more money.
  • Caregivers: spouses and adult children, mostly women. Sandwich generation: caring for children/grandchildren and elders.
  • US: private solutions to collective problems; ambivalence between obligation and individualism, which hides intergenerational help. Divorce reduces support; stepfamily support depends on closeness.

Dykstra (2021) optional

  • Four arrangements: living alone, with spouse only, intergenerational co-residence, institutional care.
  • Living alone comes from diverse life courses. Three explanations: demographic (fewer children), economic (income enables independence), cultural (individualism). Living alone ≠ vulnerable; "near co-residence".
  • Co-residence: more in southern and eastern Europe, less in the north; declined but reversed in crises. Advantages: companionship, support; disadvantages: less autonomy, conflict, overcrowding.
  • Institutional care: "limited convergence"; re-familialisation (passive withdrawal or active cash-for-care) and marketisation → risk of dualisation of care.
  • "Ageing in place" vs simply "staying put": services must exist for home to be adequate.

Moen, Lam & Jackson (2014) optional

  • Three trends create "older families": longer, healthier lives; ageing Boomers (1946–64); delayed and declining marriage and fertility.
  • No "normal" family course; later life is heterogeneous.
  • Institutions built on the breadwinner/homemaker model shape older families; shift from defined-benefit to defined-contribution pensions.
  • Couples face two retirements; retirement is a family transition, not individual (linked lives). Families as "role budgeting centers" (Goode); bridge jobs as gender strategies; couples prioritise the husband's job.
  • Primary, secondary, tertiary caregivers; women (wives, then daughters) as main carers.

Hämäläinen & Tanskanen (2021), "'Sandwich generation': generational transfers towards adult children and elderly parents"

Journal of Family Studies 27(3), 336–355

Finnish baby boomers invest more in their adult children than in their elderly parents (emotional closeness, care, money), except for practical help, which goes more to parents. Women give more care, men more practical help, and support favours daughters and mothers.

Main ideas

  • Longevity and later childbearing mean people in their 60s may face demands both upward (elderly parents) and downward (adult children).
  • Three meanings of "sandwich generation": caring for dependent children and parents at once (often women); a middle generation among several; here, anyone with at least one living parent and one adult child.
  • Data: Gentrans survey 2012, Finnish boomers born 1945–50 (aged 62–67); 360 respondents with a living parent and adult child; family fixed-effect regressions comparing transfers within the same family.
  • Four transfers: financial aid, practical help, care (personal care for parents, grandchild care for children), emotional closeness.
  • Findings: emotional closeness, care and money go more to adult children; practical help more to parents (parents' needs; skills like helping with technology).
  • Gender: women emotionally closer and give more care to both generations; men give more practical help to children. Daughters favoured over sons; women closer to and care more for mothers than fathers.
  • Finland matters: a generous Nordic welfare state with no legal duty to support kin, so transfers are more "voluntary".

Concepts and theories tested

Kin selection theory (Hamilton)Invest in close relatives.
Reproductive value hypothesisInvesting in younger kin pays off more → downward flow.
Intergenerational stake hypothesis (Bengtson)Parents care more about continuity than children → downward flow.
Kin keeper theoryWomen provide more transfers, especially care.
Matrilateral effectMore support to daughters and mothers.
Need and opportunity structures (Szydlik)Needs of parents can override downward tendency.

Retelling

The authors note that most studies of intergenerational help look at two generations at a time and come from English-speaking countries. They ask what happens when a person sits between living parents and adult children: who gets more? Using a nationally representative Finnish survey of baby boomers, they compare, within each family, what the same person gives to their parent and to their child.

The flow is mostly downward: boomers feel closer to their children, help them more with grandchildcare and give them money far more often (41% vs 4%). Only practical help (chores, repairs, paperwork, technology) goes more to the very old parents, who need it. Women are more emotionally involved and give more care; men give more practical help. Both sexes favour daughters, and women favour their mothers. The results fit evolutionary (reproductive value) and sociological (intergenerational stake) explanations and kin-keeper and matrilateral predictions, in a welfare state where help is largely a choice.

Powell (2018), "Care for older people in multigenerational families: a life course analysis across four generations"

Families, Relationships and Societies 7(1), 103–121

Four-generation families organise care for frail elders through ongoing negotiation guided by an ethics of care, combining family and professional support to balance autonomy, privacy and needs.

Main ideas

  • Finch: family members feel a moral obligation to care; Finch & Mason: commitments are negotiated. Morality is sensitivity to others, not only fixed rules.
  • Older people decide how best to look after themselves given their family's circumstances; generations now overlap for many years.
  • Care options form a continuum: home-based family care; family care + professional help; sheltered housing + family care; institutional care for chronic conditions.
  • Geography and time matter: needs, relations, policies and capacity change, so support is constantly redesigned.
  • Sharing care avoids "burdening" one person; families give emotional support, professionals practical care.
  • Institutional care was the least desired option (deinstitutionalisation policies, poor perceived quality, feeling of abandonment).
  • Decisions follow an ethics of care, not consumer choice; a trustworthy source of support matters more than choice.

Mandatory · seminarOpen questionSummary from slides

UN Women (2019), Ch. 5: Long-term care for older persons, women's right to receive care

Population ageing raises long-term care needs everywhere, but care models relying only on families are unsustainable, and women are both the main unpaid carers and the most vulnerable care receivers.

Main ideas

  • Functional ability declines with age; needs vary because of cumulative life-course deprivations.
  • Family-only care is unsustainable: migration separates generations, fertility decline means fewer children, women's employment limits full-time caregiving.
  • Women are the majority of older people; they marry older men and live longer, so they care for spouses but are less often cared for by one, ending up in care homes.
  • Unpaid carers (spouses, daughters, daughters-in-law) are the "invisible backbone" of long-term care systems; their health suffers; burdens fall on less powerful members.
  • After the death of the person cared for, carers may lose access to income, pensions, survivor benefits or inheritance.

When home is where the harm is

Family violence is shaped by the normalisation of violence, separate-spheres privacy, family norms and structural inequalities. Definitions are contextual, measurement underestimates, and policy responses can protect victims while over-surveilling the marginalised.

Key points for the multiple choice

What the slides say

Context and definitions

  • COVID lockdowns raised domestic violence (more exposure, fewer resources, more stress). Reports of child abuse fell because schools closed, though fewer reports could also mean relief from over-surveillance of families of colour.
  • Three most-studied types: child abuse and neglect, intimate partner violence, elder abuse.
  • Violence is normalised in American society (media, corporal punishment legal in 19 states, banned in only 2; high homicide and violent crime, inequality).
  • Separate spheres make violence a private matter and give men authority as household heads. Interlocking inequalities raise risk (Native American women, undocumented migrant women).
  • The meaning of violence is contextual: condemned or accepted depending on who acts, who suffers, and where. Less severe violence by powerful institutions (governments, schools) stays hidden.
  • WHO definition: intentional use of physical force or power, threatened or actual, likely to cause injury, death, psychological harm, maldevelopment or deprivation. Jackman: violent acts can be verbal, written or physical; injuries physical, psychological, social, material.
  • Social conditions of risk: family organisation (time together, power differences), idealisation of the family as a safe private place, family norms (e.g. "good housekeeper" expectations), cultural normalisation, structural inequalities (racism, sexism, classism, nativism, ageism, colonialism).
  • Measurement underestimates: violence is hidden; victims may not recognise or disclose it. Forms of family violence are interconnected (shared consequences, risk and protective factors).

Child abuse and neglect

  • Acts of commission or omission by a caregiver causing harm or threat of harm: neglect, physical, sexual, emotional abuse.
  • Women are more often abusers (more time with children), except sexual abuse.
  • Higher reported rates in poor families: fewer resources (neglect charges), social isolation, closer institutional scrutiny (especially poor Black and Indigenous families).
  • ACEs (adverse childhood experiences): abuse, witnessing violence, instability, separation, family member with mental illness, substance abuse or incarceration; lead to reduced education/work, substance abuse, health problems, poor maternal health.
  • Policy history: 1874 Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children; 1900s criminalising laws; 1960s mandatory reporting laws (teachers, doctors), which also increased surveillance of marginalised families.
  • Foster care: temporary placement; goal = reunification; most stay under 2 years. Indian Child Welfare Act: tribal authority, Indigenous placement priority (uneven enforcement).
  • UN Convention on the Rights of the Child: children are rights-holders, not property. Four principles: non-discrimination; best interests; survival and development; right to be heard. US has not ratified it.

Elder abuse

  • Intentional act or failure to act causing risk of harm to an adult 60+: physical, sexual, emotional/psychological, neglect, financial abuse.
  • Risk: social isolation, dependency; older elders, women, those with a history of abuse.
  • Perpetrators: often adult children or spouses, with substance abuse, health problems, police trouble, isolation, unemployment, stress.
  • Policy: 1974 Social Security Act (Adult Protective Services), mandatory reporting in all states, Elder Justice Act 2010 (part of the Affordable Care Act), not fully implemented.

Intimate partner violence

  • IPV: violence in a current or former romantic relationship: physical, sexual (incl. non-physical like sexting without consent), stalking, psychological/emotional aggression. Occurs in dating, marriage, cohabitation.
  • Risks: individual (alcohol, belief in strict gender roles, witnessing violence as a child), interpersonal (conflict, possessiveness, economic stress), societal (poverty, low support; societies condoning violence with inequality and weak safety nets).
  • Men more often perpetrators, women more often victims.
  • Coercive controlling violence / intimate terrorism: violence to control a partner (domination, isolation, intimidation); clear gender asymmetry.
  • Violent resistance: self-defence against a violent controlling partner.
  • Situational couple violence: violence in conflicts without a pattern of control; gender symmetry; can be minor or dangerous.
  • Teen dating violence: psychological more prevalent than physical; cyber abuse; predicts later IPV.
  • VAWA (1994): funding, protection for undocumented women, Native Nations' authority. Mandatory arrest laws have unintended effects: non-disclosure, fear of escalation, custody loss, criminalisation of marginalised groups.

Eggers, Grages & Höppner (2026), "Addressing social risks of older people with care needs: how European welfare states combine pension and long-term care policies"

Journal of Social Policy 55(3), 739–756

Older people with care needs face two linked risks: poverty and unmet care. How well a welfare state protects them depends on the combination of its pension and long-term care (LTC) generosity, which yields four institutional constellations.

Main ideas

  • Ageing pressures welfare states: most expanded LTC rights, while pension reforms shifted responsibility to private actors. Little research looks at the two fields together.
  • Old social risks (pensions designed for uninterrupted male careers) vs new social risks (interrupted female careers, atypical work, less family care, LTC needs).
  • Typology (pension generosity × LTC generosity): complementary (both high), pension-oriented supplementary (high pension, low LTC), LTC-oriented supplementary (high LTC, low pension), residual (both low).
  • Method: comparative case study of Denmark, England, Germany, Italy, Poland (one per regime type); generosity measured institutionally by access and benefit level for each field (document analysis of laws, MISSOC, OECD, EUROCARERS).
  • Scores: pensions: Denmark 5, Italy 5, Germany 3, Poland 3, England 1. LTC: Denmark 7, Germany 5, Italy 3, England 3, Poland 1.
  • Results: Denmark = complementary (de-familialisation + supported familialism); England = residual (targets the very poor; marginally supported familialism); Poland = residual (unsupported familialism; family care first); Italy = pension-oriented supplementary (generous pensions; care by family or undeclared live-in migrant carers); Germany = LTC-oriented supplementary (generous universal LTC, weaker pensions), the one surprise against regime expectations.
  • Only Denmark fully covers both risks independent of wealth and family; elsewhere families (mostly women) or private money fill gaps, with poverty and inequality risks.

Sociological concepts

Welfare regimes (Esping-Andersen)
Old vs new social risks (Bonoli; Taylor-Gooby)
Institutional constellations / complementarity (Deeg)
Generosity: access + benefit level
De-familialisation, supported and unsupported familialism (Saraceno)
Semi-formal care (paid family/neighbour care)

Retelling

The authors start from a gap: pensions and long-term care are usually studied separately, yet an older person who needs care may have to pay for it out of a pension. They ask how European welfare states differ in combining the two. They build a two-by-two typology and score five countries on legal rules: how easy it is to access each benefit and how generous it is.

Denmark protects both income and care, offering free home care and paid family care. England and Poland leave large gaps: England helps mainly the very poor, Poland expects families to care with little support. Italy pays good pensions but provides little care, so families or undeclared migrant carers fill the gap. Germany, surprisingly for a conservative regime, offers generous universal care insurance but weaker pensions, so poorer elders may use care cash as income. The lesson is that protection against old-age risks depends on how policies fit together; where they do not, the burden falls on families and private resources.

Ronald, Lau & Chan (2026), "Housing opportunities and family reciprocity: intergenerational support for young people and the role of housing and welfare policy in Hong Kong"

As public housing support wanes and prices soar, responsibility for housing young adults is shifting from the state back to parents. This amounts to a renegotiation of the "generational contract" towards interpersonal transfers within the family.

Main ideas

  • Young people struggle to live independently: high prices, tighter rental conditions, decline of social housing, job insecurity.
  • Post-war generations reached homeownership easily; it was part of a "successful" life course and gave socio-economic security. Middle-class parents' ownership now shapes adult children's life chances.
  • Hong Kong has a productivist welfare system: social policies support working, self-reliant households to drive economic growth. Public renting and homeownership schemes used to sustain high household welfare.
  • Across East Asia, families first pooled resources to buy housing (late 20th c.), then co-residence and parental help to buy (often at marriage) rose. Despite expectations that the young serve their elders, older cohorts now carry housing-market pressures.
  • Findings: young people stay much longer in the parental home and buy late or never. Parents are concerned but sceptical of direct financial transfers (few resources; children should be responsible for themselves): they are "conservative givers". Some co-reside to support themselves as much as their children.
  • The government is seen as responsible for youth housing, yet welfare is stigmatised. Co-residence in public flats reflects filial values but also gives older people low-cost housing, care and income.

Sociological concepts

Generational contract and its renegotiation
Productivist welfare regime (East Asia)
Housing as a welfare pillar
Intergenerational transfers and reciprocity
Co-residence and filial values
Re-familialisation of risk

Retelling

In Hong Kong, housing used to be the hidden backbone of welfare: public rental flats and subsidised ownership let families achieve security during fast economic growth. Today flats are unaffordable and public schemes have shrunk, so young adults stay at home longer and many may never own. The authors show that parents worry, but they are cautious "conservative givers" with limited means who expect children to stand on their own. Co-residence becomes a two-way arrangement that helps the young and the old.

The state is still seen as responsible, yet asking for welfare is stigmatised. The family and the state are being rebalanced as guarantors of housing and welfare security, and the burden is moving back onto families.

The logic behind the questions

Structure

  • Section I: 25 multiple-choice questions, then Section II: one open question worth 5 points.
  • Questions follow the course order, about 4–6 per week: Q1–5 weeks 1–2, Q6–11 theories (W2), Q12–16 parenting (W6), Q17–19 transitions to adulthood (W4), Q20–25 unions (W5).

  • Quiz 1 said "multiple answers might be correct". Quiz 2 instructions say one right answer: pick the best one.

Six question types

  1. Definition → term: "Which type of kin includes grandparents, parents and children?" (lineal).
  2. Description → framework/concept: "This sociological framework explores how family experiences change over a lifetime…" (life course).
  3. Author attribution: "According to Parsons and Bales…", "According to Giddens…", "Lareau identified…".
  4. NOT / EXCEPT: "Which is not a benchmark…", "What is not one of the tenets of intensive mothering?"
  5. Historical characterisation: "How were women's legal rights characterised during institutional marriage?"
  6. True / False testing the sociological stance (5 of 25 on Quiz 1).

How the distractors work

  • Wrong options are neighbouring concepts from the same slide (lineal vs collateral vs voluntary vs chosen kin; the four frameworks reused across questions).
  • One option is usually obviously absurd ("Having a gap year to travel around the world", "Not loving children as much", "Free, happy, flexible").
  • Another is the opposite of the right answer (e.g. "Early, quick, linear, synchronised" vs "Postponed, desynchronised, reversible").
  • Watch absolute words: always, never, no, superior, similar for everyone.

True/False rule of thumb

  • Statements claiming one culture or form is superior, or that experiences are the same across social locations, are False ("Western love marriages are superior…", "pathway to adulthood looks similar…").
  • Statements that history was always liberal are usually False ("Western countries have centuries-long traditions of divorce for incompatibility" – divorce was prohibited until the 1950s–60s).
  • Statements that structure, policy or social location shape family life are usually True ("parenting is shaped at individual, interactional and institutional levels").
